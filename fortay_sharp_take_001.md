A buyer walked us through their pipeline last month. Ten deals north of £100,000 in annual contract value. Seven of them had been at proposal stage for 90 days. Two for over six months. The biggest, a national retailer, had a £25,000 a month NICE saving on the table and still had not closed.

The CFO had signed it off. The Head of CX had signed it off. The procurement team had signed it off. Everybody wanted to do it. Nobody was doing it.

This is the most common pattern we see in mid market UK sales right now, and the cost case is not what is closing it.

## The £25k saving was real

NICE was charging the retailer £75,000 a month. Replacing it dropped the line to £50,000. That is £300,000 a year. The maths was clean, the assumptions were defensible, the procurement team had pressure tested the numbers. There was no challenge on the saving.

There was every other kind of challenge.

The Head of IT was worried about the migration window. The Head of CX was worried about adoption. The Group Operations Director was worried about contract overlap. The CFO was worried about the second year price (because every platform commitment renews into a higher rate at year three). The legal team was worried about data residency.

A £25,000 a month saving does not solve any of those worries. It just makes the buyer feel slightly worse about not closing.

## Cost is permission to talk, not a reason to sign

I will say this twice because it changes how we sell. Cost is permission to talk. Cost is not a reason to sign.

A finance director will not authorise a £300,000 a year platform migration on the strength of the saving alone. They will authorise it on the strength of three things together. The saving. A named outcome they recognise from a peer in their sector. And a credible story about what month two looks like, because the implementation risk is what they actually fear.

Most platform sales decks contain only the first one. They are a saving in search of a buyer.

## What actually closed the deal

Three of the seven stuck deals from that pipeline have moved in the last six weeks. None of them moved on the cost number. They moved on something else.

The retailer needed a peer story. We sent them an anonymised case study of a 2,000 person retail business that had moved off NICE in 14 weeks. Same starting point, named buyer, named outcome at month nine. The Group Operations Director read it on a Tuesday. The contract was signed on the Friday.

A wealth manager needed implementation comfort. The Head of IT had been bitten by a previous Avaya migration that took 14 months. We wrote a one page document called "what week 9 looks like, not week 1". It described what the team would be doing 90 days after go live. Specific, named, dated. He printed it. He took it to the steering group. He stopped blocking.

A logistics business needed a CFO conversation. Their CFO was the actual obstacle, not the IT team. We did not send another saving model. We sent a one page risk register that named every operational risk associated with the migration and the named mitigation per risk. CFOs do not buy savings. They buy reduced risk.

## So what do we do about it

Three things change in how the proposal stage is built, if you want this to stop happening.

First, write the proposal in two halves. The cost half is the back of the document. The front of the document is the named peer outcome and the implementation risk register. The buyer is going to read the front, then check the maths in the back. If the front does not exist, the maths is unread.

Second, name the people who have done it before. Not "a leading UK retailer". A 2,000 person retailer at peak season hitting CSAT 4.6, named human as champion, named platform, named timeline. We have done that work for our customers. We know it works. Most consultancies and platform sales teams are still hiding behind anonymisation that smells like fiction.

Third, write the implementation risk register before the buyer asks for it. Put it on page two. Show the named mitigation per risk. The CFO will know within ten seconds of opening the document whether you have thought about this seriously. Most proposals fail this test.

## The cost number is the easy bit

If you are sitting in a pipeline review wondering why the proposals with clear ROI have not closed, the answer is almost never the ROI. It is that the buyer cannot get internal alignment, and your proposal is not helping them do that.

Cost is permission to talk. Peer outcomes get the meeting. Implementation comfort gets the signature.

Stop sending savings models. Start sending peer stories with risk registers.

That is what closes a £25,000 a month deal.
